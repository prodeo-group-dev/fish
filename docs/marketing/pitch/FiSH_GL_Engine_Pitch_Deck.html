02The Problem

Capital is already moving into this region. It has nowhere compliant to land.

Most businesses across this region run on paper, spreadsheets, or software built for a market with different tax law, different currency reality, and different audit expectations entirely. Meanwhile the capital is real: Sierra Leone imports over $160M of rice a year despite 5.4 million hectares of arable land, 75% of it uncultivated — regionally, ECOWAS imports roughly $3.5B of rice annually against a shortfall the bloc has committed $24B to closing by 2035. That capital still needs somewhere compliant to land.

The World Bank's own review of Sierra Leone's agri-finance sector is blunt about why capital doesn't convert into credit: it isn't a shortage of capital or ambition, it's a shortage of trustworthy financial data. The government's own MUNAFA Fund reached over 6,800 SMEs, over 70% of them women-led — yet a national survey found only 8.5% of all firms have ever received any formal microfinance, attributed to poor targeting and fragmented delivery. The same gap - a business with no books a lender, auditor, or tax authority can actually trust - exists well beyond agrifinance, in every sector this region's businesses operate in.

Nigeria is the contrast that sharpens this — same missing layer, a completely different market around it:

Mano River

Capital is real and already committed — $24B pledged through the ECOWAS Rice Roadmap. No compliant ledger exists here at all. Uncontested ground, ready to enter now.

Nigeria

Capital and demand are already proven — a mature, vibrant market, the largest in West Africa. Fintech exists here (Opay among others), but none of it is a compliant, IFRS-grounded ledger. Heavily contested — worth entering only once, and only ready.

The connective tissue missing isn't more capital. It's a ledger these businesses, and the institutions financing them, can actually trust — one that speaks the region's own tax law, not a generic import from a market it was never built for.

04The Solution

FiSH — a General Ledger Engine built to be someone else's infrastructure.

Not another bookkeeping app, but GLaaS — General Ledger as a Service: a multi-tenant, API-first ledger engine — double-entry, audited, IFRS-aligned — designed from its first line of code to run four jurisdictions' tax law and a dozen business types on one shared core.

Cloud-native and service-oriented, not a monolith: the ledger core, sales, purchasing, inventory, and HR/payroll are each built as independently deployable services, not modules bolted onto one codebase. The ledger core is live on AWS today; the rest are built and tested to the same standard, ready to deploy on the same pattern — the same architecture proven infrastructure companies run at scale, not a prototype's shortcut.

Real double-entry ledger

Chart of Accounts, period locking, full audit trail, multi-currency (SLE/GBP/USD/EUR and more).

Built-in tax engine

Corporate income tax, GST/VAT/TVA, and payroll levies computed per-jurisdiction — SL, Liberia, Guinea, Côte d'Ivoire, plus UK, Ireland, Nigeria already modelled.

IFRS-grounded reporting

Trial Balance, Balance Sheet, P&L, Cash Flow, AR/AP ageing, ECL provisioning, fixed-asset depreciation — not spreadsheet approximations.

API-first, multi-tenant

Every capability exposed as a clean service boundary — the same engine is already built to carry four internal product lines, not just one.

This is the magnet: not persuading capital to move, just giving it somewhere compliant to land.

05Who This Reaches

People should be able to run their business from their phone.

The phone in a smallholder farmer's pocket carries more computing power than the guidance computer that flew Apollo 11 to the moon. The barrier was never capability — it was software built for a smartphone and a bank branch neither exists for most of this market.

FiSH's own interface is a mobile-first progressive web app — no app-store install, works on an ordinary phone browser, built for a single-column screen first and widened from there. Platform-agnostic by design: iOS or Android doesn't matter, because there's no native app to fragment across either one. Underneath it, the engine is API-first: any business built on FiSH chooses its own access layer for its own customers — web, mobile, USSD, whatever actually reaches them — without the ledger itself needing to change.

Design the ledger to not care how a business's customers reach it, and the addressable market stops being "who can afford a smartphone" and starts being whoever that business decides to serve.

Outgrower schemes, commodity buyers, and lenders are simply where the first paying relationships start.

This compounds, it doesn't just stack: Sales and Purchase Order Processing already model two FiSH tenants transacting directly with each other, not just each one privately booking its own side of the deal — and every eOrder or eInvoice one of them sends reaches a real counterparty who isn't a tenant yet, the transaction itself doing the introducing. At scale, that's the target — 1M business ledgers across Africa, each a counterparty another tenant can trust and trade with, at £5–£10 per ledger per month — priced for economies of scale, not per-customer margin (GBP — Prodeo Capital's own reporting currency; regional market figures elsewhere in this deck are USD, matching their original sources). That's £60M–£120M in annual recurring revenue once the network, not just the software, is doing the selling. Priced as an investment, not a cost: £5–£10 a month replaces the 2–5 back-office staff a business would otherwise need for the same bookkeeping, compliance, and reporting work — a rounding error next to that headcount, and next to what staying invisible to every lender, auditor, and tax authority costs on top of it.

13Go-to-Market — Phase 1

Four real markets — Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea, Côte d'Ivoire — with a customer pipeline Prodeo Capital already sources.

Prodeo Capital's role is bringing businesses onto FiSH — a UK-regulated credit union is the clearest proof point, its requirements already modelled in the platform (Chart of Accounts, tenancy model, and onboarding flow all built). It isn't a live tenant yet: it comes on board once FiSH is proven, the same sequencing as any other business Prodeo Capital brings onto the platform, not a privileged internal case. That proof of capability — a real regulated business's ledger requirements, already modelled in code — is what de-risks every customer conversation that follows, ahead of any of them going live.

Institutional relationships already identified as entry points: SMEDA (coordinating/advisory), the MUNAFA Fund (as a channel for its own lending capital, targeted more effectively with real data), and ICASL (standards recognition that gives FiSH's output credibility with banks and auditors). Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea, and Côte d'Ivoire all run on it — their tax rules are already modelled, not a future build.

One example of what that channel work looks like in practice: the Mano River Union's AfDB-backed $4.2M grant already funding women cross-border traders in Liberia and Sierra Leone to formalise — a cohort with income to protect and no record-keeping tool of its own. Not yet a partnership, but exactly the kind of funded, ready-made audience this region keeps producing.

This region earns its own revenue and its own relationships — it isn't a warm-up act. It's also where FiSH builds the operating muscle a bigger, far more contested market won't wait around for: get to Nigeria undercooked, and entrenched players simply run you out. That's the clock this raise is racing: real operating experience here has to be in hand before Nigeria's 2027 window opens, not started after it.

14Go-to-Market — Phase 2

Nigeria decides scale — and punishes an unready entrant fastest.

West Africa's largest economy and its most contested digital-finance market. Mobile-money and neobank players — Opay among them — have already proven the demand for digital financial infrastructure at scale, and they will defend that ground hard. None of them are built as a compliant, IFRS-grounded ledger engine underneath a regulated business's own books — but arriving with an unproven product against incumbents that size doesn't get a second attempt.

The tax engine isn't starting from zero here either: the Nigeria Tax Act 2025 (effective 2026-01-01) — a flat 30% CIT with a turnover-and-fixed-assets small-company exemption, plus a separate 4% Development Levy — is already modelled, with employer pension contributions flagged as under active PenCom review rather than guessed past.

The sequencing is the strategy: enter Nigeria carrying real operating experience from four other markets, not a first production deployment — timed past Nigeria's 2027 general election, when tax enforcement typically tightens, not before it.
